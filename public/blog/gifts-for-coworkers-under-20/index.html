What makes a good gift for a coworker?

A good coworker gift is small, useful during the workday, and impossible to misread. You are buying for someone you see five days a week but may not know well outside the building, so the gift has two jobs: make their Tuesday slightly better, and say nothing that could be awkward in front of the rest of the team.

Three filters do most of the work. First, would they use it at their desk, on the commute, or in the break room? Second, could they open it in a meeting without anyone raising an eyebrow? Third, does it cost about the same as what everyone else is spending? If a gift passes all three, it is almost certainly fine.

Workplace advice columnist Alison Green puts the boundary in plain terms in her rules for office gift-giving at Ask a Manager: avoid gifts that are worn or put on the body, such as perfume, lotion, clothes and jewelry, because they read as too personal, and treat gag gifts as risky. Food is the safe default, as long as you keep allergies in mind. That one paragraph rules out a surprising share of what the big coworker lists recommend.

The ideas below are grouped by the kind of coworker, because "a coworker" is not one person. The one who is always cold wants something different from the one who runs on tea, and $20 goes further when it is aimed.

Coworker gifts under $20, by type of coworker

Every pick here usually sells for under $20 on Amazon, though prices move around in December, so check before you add to cart.

The note-taker who is never without a pen

Some people take notes in every meeting and quietly judge the free pens in the supply closet. Field Notes Original Kraft memo books come in a three-pack that slips into a back pocket or a laptop sleeve, and they look like something a person chose rather than something the office ordered. Pair them with a pack of Uni-ball Jetstream RT retractable pens, a hybrid ink ballpoint that writes darker and smudges less than a basic office pen, which matters to anyone who writes fast and closes the notebook right away.

The coworker who is always cold

Every office has someone in a sweater in July. A box of HotHands hand warmers is a practical, slightly funny gift that gets used: open a packet, shake it, and it stays warm for hours on the walk from the parking lot or under a cold desk. It costs little, nobody has too many, and it says you noticed something real about them.

The tea drinker with a mug that never leaves their desk

Harney & Sons Hot Cinnamon Spice tea is a strong, sweet-smelling black tea that tastes like the holidays without any added sugar, and the tin looks good on a shelf after the tea is gone. For the hot chocolate person, Ghirardelli Double Chocolate hot cocoa mix is a clear step up from the packets in the break room.

The coworker with three devices and no charger

A spare cable is the least glamorous gift on this list and possibly the most used. An Anker USB-C to USB-C cable in a longer length reaches from an outlet under the desk to a phone on top of it, which the short cable in the box never does. Add a bottle of Whoosh! screen cleaner, which cleans smudges off phones, laptops and monitors, and you have a tidy little desk kit.

The one whose desk could use some life

A small live plant is a gift that keeps going after the holidays. A Costa Farms succulent or a small snake plant survives fluorescent light, weekends and long stretches of being ignored, which is exactly the care schedule most office plants get. Check first whether your office allows plants and whether the person has a desk of their own rather than a shared one.

The whole team, on one budget

If you want to give something to a group of six or ten people without buying six or ten gifts, buy one thing to share. A bag of Ghirardelli Peppermint Bark squares or Lindt Lindor assorted truffles on the team table, with a short note, is generous and easy. For a team that eats lunch together, a deck of UNO cards left in the break room tends to get more use than anyone expects.

How much should you spend on a coworker gift?

Spend whatever the group is spending, and if there is no group norm, keep it modest. Around $10 to $20 is the usual range for a peer you work alongside, and less is fine for a quick thank-you or a big team. If your office runs an exchange with a cap, stay right at the cap. Going well over it puts the person who gets your name in an awkward spot, and going well under it reads as careless.

Direction matters too. The long-standing advice in Green's Ask a Manager post is that gifts should flow down, not up: a manager can give to their team, but employees should not feel they need to buy for the boss. If you want to thank a manager, a sincere card does the job. If the team wants to give something, a shared card or one small group gift keeps it fair for people on tighter budgets.

What to avoid giving coworkers

Most coworker gifts go wrong in predictable ways, and the predictable ways are easy to dodge.

  1. Anything worn or put on the body. Lotion, perfume, clothing and jewelry feel personal at work, even when the intent is kind. Many popular lists still lead with scented lotion and novelty mugs; older roundups like this one from The Muse show how common that is. Skip the body products.
  2. Alcohol, unless you know for certain. Some coworkers do not drink for health, religious or personal reasons, and a bottle on their desk puts them in an awkward position.
  3. Jokes that need context. A gag gift can land well with one person and badly with the person sitting next to them. If it needs an explanation, leave it out.
  4. Food with obvious allergens, unlabeled. Homemade cookies are lovely, but label anything with nuts, and keep packaging on store-bought treats so people can read the ingredients.
  5. Uneven gifts. If you are giving to several people on the same team, give them all something similar. People compare, even when they say they do not.

When you barely know the coworker

Drawing a name you only know from email signatures is the hardest version of this. In that case, choose something nearly everyone uses and nobody minds getting a second of: good pens, a notebook, a nice tea or cocoa, or hand warmers in winter. These are pleasant to receive and easy to pass on if they are not quite right.

If you have a week, look for one small clue. What mug do they carry into meetings? Do they bike in? Do they keep a sweater on the chair? Frequently asked questions

What is a good gift for a coworker under $20?

A good coworker gift under $20 is something they will use during the workday, such as a pack of Field Notes memo books with good pens, a tin of Harney & Sons tea, hand warmers for a cold office, or a long USB-C cable. Pick by what you have seen them use at work, and avoid anything personal like lotion or clothing.

Is it okay to give coworkers food as a gift?

Yes. Food is one of the safest coworker gifts because it gets used up and rarely feels personal. Choose store-bought treats with the ingredient label still on, or clearly label anything homemade that contains nuts or other common allergens, so people with allergies can decide for themselves.

Should I buy my boss a holiday gift?

Usually not. The common workplace etiquette is that gifts flow down, so a manager may give to their team, but employees are not expected to buy for the boss. A sincere handwritten card is the better way to say thanks. If the team wants to do more, one small group gift keeps it fair for everyone.

How much should I spend on a coworker gift?

Match whatever your team is spending. Without a group norm, about $10 to $20 is typical for a peer, and less is fine for large teams or quick thank-yous. If there is an exchange with a price cap, stay right at the cap rather than far over or under it, so nobody feels outdone.

What should I give coworkers I do not know well?

Give something nearly everyone uses and nobody minds a spare of, such as nice pens, a pocket notebook, a good tea or hot cocoa, or a pack of hand warmers in winter. These are pleasant to receive, easy to pass along if they miss, and carry no risk of feeling too personal.
