What to get someone who says they don't want anything

Updated November 28, 2026 · 8 min read

Short answer

What to get someone who says they don't want anything: something they will use up, something that replaces what they already use daily, or an experience with a date attached, never a new object to store. First work out why they said it: cost, clutter, no ideas, or dislike of fuss. Then match the gift to that reason and hand it over without ceremony.

What do people mean when they say they don't want anything?

"Don't get me anything." It is the least helpful sentence in gift-giving, and almost everyone says it at some point. The problem is not that it is a lie; it usually is not. The problem is that it answers a different question than the one you asked.

You asked what they want. They answered whether they need anything. Those are not the same, and treating them as the same is how you end up in a store on the last afternoon before a birthday, holding a candle you do not believe in.

Before you buy anything, work out which version of "nothing" you are dealing with. In practice there are four, and they call for different responses.

What they sayWhat is usually underneathWhat works
"Don't spend money on me"Guilt about cost, or a sense that money is tight for youSomething inexpensive and clearly thoughtful, or something you made
"I don't need more stuff"Real clutter fatigue, often someone in a small home or in the middle of a declutterConsumables, experiences, or a replacement for something they own
"I honestly can't think of anything"They are not a wants-driven person and find the question stressfulFix a small annoyance in their daily routine
"Really, nothing, I mean it"Dislikes being the center of attention or feeling indebtedSomething small, given casually, with no ceremony

Only one of those four is about money. The most common is the second: the person is not being humble, they are being practical. They have looked at the shelf in their hallway and decided another decorative object is a burden, not a treat.

The question that sorts it out

Ask something different and listen to the shape of the answer. Not "what do you want?" but "what's been annoying you lately?" People who cannot name a want can almost always name an irritation. A dead kettle, a bag strap that digs in, a pan everything sticks to. That is your gift, and it arrives disguised as small talk.

Five kinds of gift that work on people who want nothing

The unifying rule: do not add an object to their life. Every category below either gets used up, replaces something, or takes up no space at all.

1. Consumables they would never buy at that quality

The most reliable category. Good coffee, real olive oil, excellent chocolate, an interesting hot sauce, decent tea. They will finish it, so it can never become clutter, and the version you buy is nicer than the one they buy for themselves, so it registers as a treat rather than groceries. A bottle of Graza olive oil in its squeeze bottles is a good example: a pantry staple, just better than the one in their cabinet.

The mistake is the hamper of twelve things. One excellent item beats a basket of adequate ones. A single bar of really good chocolate says "I know what you like." A gift basket says "I bought a gift basket."

2. The upgrade to something they already use every day

This is the strongest category, and it is underused because it feels boring to the giver. It is not boring to the person receiving it. Look at what they touch every morning and buy the better version.

  • They make coffee every day: a Baratza Encore burr grinder, which Wirecutter's testing, as republished by Engadget, called "the best grinder for most people," or a Fellow Stagg EKG kettle, which Coffee Review rated 9.5 and called "best in show".
  • They cook constantly: one really good chef's knife. The Victorinox Fibrox Pro 8-inch chef's knife has been a long-running favorite in America's Test Kitchen's tests of chef's knives under $75, and it replaces a dull one instead of joining it.
  • They walk the dog at 6 a.m.: a warm hat that is actually warm, like a Smartwool merino beanie, not a novelty one.

Because it replaces something rather than adding to it, the "no more stuff" objection disappears. You have made their pile of possessions better without making it bigger. Our guide to gifts for someone who has everything goes further down this road.

3. Maintenance for things they love

Deeply underrated. If someone owns a good leather bag, a cast iron pan, a bike, a record player, or boots they have had for a decade, the thing that keeps that object alive is a gift that respects what they already chose. A bottle of Bickmore Bick 4 leather conditioner, a Lodge chainmail scrubber for the cast iron, a bike chain cleaning kit, or a resole from a local cobbler.

The message is unusually good: I noticed what you care about, and I want it to last. That is a hard sentiment to buy directly, and this category buys it for under $20.

4. Experiences with a date attached

Experiences avoid the clutter problem completely, but they have a failure mode: the voucher that sits in a drawer until it expires, which quietly becomes a chore you gave someone. The fix is to attach a date and do the planning yourself.

"A cooking class sometime" is a burden. "We're doing a pasta class on the 14th, it's booked, I've told your partner, wear something you don't mind getting flour on" is a gift. The work you removed is the present.

5. The thing they have mentioned twice and never bought

Everybody has one. It is usually mid-priced, mildly indulgent, and stuck in a permanent line behind more responsible purchases. A good umbrella. A weighted blanket. Decent noise-canceling headphones. These never get bought because they are never urgent, which is exactly what makes them a good gift. You are not guessing at a want; you are settling an argument they have been having with themselves.

What should you avoid buying them?

The reason someone said "nothing" matters most here, because the wrong gift confirms their fear that gift-giving is a hassle.

  • Decorative objects. Signs with words on them, ornaments, anything whose only job is to occupy a surface. This is the exact thing the clutter-averse person was warning you about.
  • Anything that needs assembly or a learning curve they did not ask for. A gift that comes with homework is a task.
  • Clothing in a size you are guessing at. Now they have to manage a return.
  • Big-ticket surprises. The person who said "don't spend money" and receives something expensive feels the debt, not the delight.
  • Gift cards to stores they don't use. That is homework with a deadline.

The honest test: in six months, will this be in use, used up, or in a closet? If the answer is closet, it is not a gift, it is a transfer of storage responsibility.

How should you give it so it doesn't feel like pressure?

This part gets ignored, and it matters more than the object. Someone who asked for nothing is often uncomfortable with the ritual, not the item. So strip the ritual out.

Give it early or give it casually. Skip the speech about how long you searched; that turns a present into an obligation, because now they have to perform gratitude in proportion to your effort. "This made me think of you" is enough. Say it, hand it over, change the subject.

If they protest anyway, one useful line: "It's not a big thing, I just wanted you to have it." That closes the loop without making them negotiate.

A worked example

Say it is your mother-in-law. She has said, firmly, that she does not need anything, she is trying to have less in the house, and please do not make a fuss.

The old approach buys a candle, and she puts it in the drawer with the other candles. This approach notices that she makes a pot of tea every afternoon and the teapot has a chipped lid, and that she mentioned in passing that the garden center stopped carrying the seeds she likes.

So: a good replacement teapot, or two packets of those seeds with a note. Small, unfussy, consumable or replacing. Nothing about it breaks a single thing she asked for, and it shows you were listening, which is the real content of any gift. The shortcut, sorted by who they are

The approach above works for anyone. But "nothing" means slightly different things depending on the relationship, and it is worth being blunt about it.

  • A parent. Usually means "don't spend your money on me." They almost always want time, or something you made or organized. Booking the thing, handling the details, or showing up with lunch beats any object.
  • A partner. Rarely literal. Often a test of whether you have been paying attention all year. Go for the thing they mentioned once in April and assumed you forgot.
  • A friend. Usually about not wanting to start an arms race of reciprocal spending. Keep it small and specific to an inside joke; the point is the recognition, not the item.
  • A coworker. Might really mean nothing. Consumable and shared is the safe zone. Our gifts for coworkers under $20 guide has picks.
  • Someone recently bereaved or unwell. Often means they have no capacity for errands. Practical help, delivered food, or a standing offer with a specific date beats anything wrapped.

When should you actually buy nothing?

Sometimes the right answer really is to respect it completely, and it deserves saying plainly because most articles skip it.

If someone has set an explicit, ongoing boundary, such as "we're not doing presents between adults anymore," that is not modesty, and overriding it is not generous. It puts them in the position of having to reciprocate next time or feel rude, which is exactly what they were trying to avoid.

The same goes for someone in financial difficulty who has quietly opted out because they cannot match it. A gift there can land as a reminder of the gap. Time, help, or a shared meal where you happen to pay is kinder than a wrapped object.

The distinction is simple: a one-off "oh, don't worry about me" is deflection. A standing arrangement is a boundary. Deflection you can gently work around. Boundaries you honor.

Frequently asked questions

Does "I don't want anything" ever actually mean nothing?

Rarely, and it is usually obvious when it does. If someone has asked to stop exchanging gifts as a standing arrangement between adults, take that at face value; it is a boundary, not modesty. The person who says it once, about one occasion, while still giving gifts themselves, is deflecting, not setting a policy.

Is it rude to buy something for someone who said not to?

Not if you respect the reason behind the refusal. A large, expensive, attention-drawing gift can feel like you overrode them. Something small, consumable or useful reads as attentive. The real failure is creating an obligation they now have to repay or store.

What if they get annoyed by the gift?

Keep the receipt and keep it low-key. Hand it over without ceremony and without a speech about how much thought you put in, because that is what creates pressure. "I saw this and thought of you, no big deal" lets them off the hook. If it is consumable, the problem solves itself within a month.

How much should I spend on someone who said not to get them anything?

Less than you normally would, on purpose. A person who said "nothing" and receives something expensive often feels indebted rather than happy. Spending about a third less than usual is a good rule: it shows you listened while still marking the occasion.

What is the single safest gift for someone who wants nothing?

An upgrade to something they already buy for themselves. Not a new category or a new hobby, just the nicer version of a thing already in their life, like a better coffee grinder or a sharper chef's knife. It cannot become clutter because it replaces something.
